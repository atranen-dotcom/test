# Startup Feasibility: Can a Non-Veterinarian Start an Emergency or Urgent-Care Vet Hospital in the Northeast (CT, NY, NJ) and Nationally?

Context: a Greenwich, CT entrepreneur (not a vet) with a 5–6 van mobile grooming business wants to know if she could start an ER or urgent-care vet business like Veterinary Emergency Group (VEG). Research date: Oct 2026. Most web sources on costs are blog or template-style and conflict with each other, and that is flagged throughout. **None of this is legal advice. Every ownership question below needs confirmation from veterinary-transaction counsel in each target state.**

## 1. Ownership rules: can a non-vet own a practice? Corporate-practice rules, MSO structures, permits, DEA

### Takeaway
Rules differ by state and the published counts disagree: anywhere from about 13 to 18 states restrict non-vet ownership. **New York is clearly restrictive**: only a vet-owned professional service corporation may practice (Educ. Law § 6706). New Jersey and Pennsylvania are usually listed as restrictive, but I could not confirm either from statute. For Connecticut I found no statutory ban on non-vet ownership, but I also could not confirm that one doesn't exist. Restrictive states are usually handled with an MSO structure: a vet-owned practice entity contracts with a management company that a lay investor owns. Even in permissive states, the facility or premises usually has to name a licensed responsible veterinarian.

### Cited Findings
- **The count of restrictive states is disputed:**
  - The American Economic Liberties Project says 18 states ban non-vet ownership. — [Economic Liberties](https://www.economicliberties.us/our-work/the-save-our-pets-act-stopping-the-corporate-takeover-of-veterinary-practices/)
  - A veterinary law firm says "roughly 15 states" allow non-vet ownership outright, but doesn't name them. — [Mahan Law](https://mahanlaw.com/practice-areas/buying-a-veterinary-practice/non-veterinarian-ownership-by-states/)
  - A guide dated Aug 2026 reviewed the statutes and says they don't support the 18 figure. Its own tally:
    - 9 jurisdictions restrict who may hold equity: 8 outright, plus North Carolina, which allows a 49% minority stake.
    - 4 allow lay ownership but bar lay control of clinical judgment.
    - 14 allow ownership if a facility permit names a responsible veterinarian.
    - "13 states write some non-veterinarian restriction into their practice act."
    - These categories add up to 27 jurisdictions, not 51. — [VeterinaryHires](https://www.veterinaryhires.com/career-guides/veterinary-practice-manager/practice-ownership/)
- **States named as restrictive:**
  - Kentucky, Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Pennsylvania, New York and North Carolina appear on restriction lists compiled by veterinary counsel. New York is called among the strictest: "only a DVM-owned professional entity may hold the practice at all." (Article dated Sept 4, 2026.) — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/); same list in [Mahan Law](https://mahanlaw.com/practice-areas/buying-a-veterinary-practice/non-veterinarian-ownership-by-states/)
  - Search summaries named New York, New Jersey, Minnesota and North Carolina as prohibiting corporate practice of veterinary medicine. — [Who Can Own a Veterinary Practice, Nidana](https://nidana.io/blog/who-can-own-a-veterinary-practice); [AGG](https://www.agg.com/news-insights/publications/vet-clinic-acquisitions-require-regulatory-diligence/) (I did not open these pages myself to confirm.)
- **States named as permissive:** Florida, Colorado, California, Virginia, Oregon, New Hampshire and Vermont.
  - California is a partial case: lay owners may employ vets, but a 2022 amendment (AB 1535) bars them from directing a vet's clinical judgment. — [Holland & Hart](https://www.hollandhart.com/navigating-corporate-veterinary-practices-in-california-a-look-at-ab-1535-and-llcs); state list from search summary of [Nidana](https://nidana.io/blog/who-can-own-a-veterinary-practice)
- **New York:**
  - Educ. Law § 6706 says: "No business corporation, other than a professional service corporation organized under the business corporation law, shall hereafter be organized for the practice of veterinary medicine." — [NY Senate, EDN § 6706](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/EDN/6706); [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2026-01-14-no-paws-oversight-will-new-yorks-proposed-veterinary)
  - Pending bill A9042 was referred to the Assembly Agriculture Committee on Jan 7, 2026. It would require written notice to the state within 14 days of agreeing to a veterinary "material change" transaction of $200,000 or more, before closing, and would let the AG challenge deals. Enactment status is unconfirmed. — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/); [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2026-01-14-no-paws-oversight-will-new-yorks-proposed-veterinary)
  - A NY law firm alert covers the rules governing sale of NY vet practices. — [RMF PC](https://rmfpc.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Veterinary-Law-Alert-Regulations-That-Govern-the-Sale-of-New-York-Veterinary-Practices.pdf)
- **New Jersey:**
  - The only explicit source found says non-vet ownership "is generally prohibited." That source is a JustAnswer Q&A and is not authoritative. — [JustAnswer](https://www.justanswer.com/business-law/ohxxd-non-veterinarian-own-practice-state-new.html)
  - I could not find the controlling N.J.A.C. 13:44 or N.J.S.A. 45:16 ownership provision.
- **Connecticut:**
  - The practice act (CGS Chapter 384, §§ 20-196 to 20-206) gives the DPH Commissioner, with the Board of Veterinary Medicine, power to issue regulations to "insure proper veterinary care." — [CGA Chapter 384](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_384.htm); [DPH statute PDF](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/departments-and-agencies/dph/dph/practitioner_licensing_and_investigations/plis/vet/vetstatutepdf.pdf?rev=93fb4b08b1994602b14aa937b3f7986c&hash=CBFD7973B5CF66906454504E0BD00E48)
  - CT facility regulations (Regs. § 20-196-1 et seq.) govern "any facility for the treatment of animals, used by a veterinarian licensed by the State." The definitions classify hospitals by veterinarian-operator type ("private or partnership hospital," "group hospital… controlled and operated by a group of veterinarians"). They appear to be decades old. — [Cornell LII § 20-196-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/connecticut/Regs-Conn-State-Agencies-SS-20-196-1); [§ 20-196-3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/connecticut/Regs-Conn-State-Agencies-SS-20-196-3)
  - One 2026 article says Connecticut had "proposed 2025 restrictions" that grandfathered existing holdings, but doesn't give enactment status. — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/)
  - CT Raised SB 1507 (2025) would restrict private equity and REIT ownership of "certain health care providers" and codify corporate-practice-of-medicine limits. The excerpts seen don't mention veterinary practices. — [CGA SB 1507](https://prdext3.cga.ct.gov/2025/TOB/S/PDF/2025SB-01507-R00-SB.PDF); [Bass Berry](https://bassberry.com/news/private-equity-reits-healthcare-laws-connecticut-maine/)
- **Texas:** 2025 bill SB 2130 defines "private equity control" of a "veterinary services provider." The search didn't confirm Texas's baseline ownership rule. — [Texas SB 2130 text](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB02130I.htm)
- **Antitrust and transaction scrutiny is rising:**
  - Colorado SB 25-198 requires 60 days' notice before a material veterinary transaction closes, and lets the AG investigate, enjoin or unwind it. — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/)
  - A 2022 FTC order requires 10-year prior approval for certain specialty and ER acquisitions within 25 miles in CA and TX. — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/)
  - Dechert notes legislation nationwide aimed at curbing corporate ownership of medical, dental and veterinary practices. — [Dechert](https://www.dechert.com/knowledge/onpoint/2025/5/healthcare-investments-flash-alert--part-i---latest-developments.html/1786614113711)
- **How the MSO structure works:**
  - A vet-owned PC holds the practice: clinical decisions, patient records and DVM employment.
  - A lay-owned MSO handles premises, equipment, staffing administration, purchasing, billing and marketing for a long-term management fee.
  - The PC leases or sells its non-medical assets to the MSO.
  - The structure draws scrutiny when management directs protocols, controls records or directs clinicians. — [VeterinaryHires](https://www.veterinaryhires.com/career-guides/veterinary-practice-manager/practice-ownership/); [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/)
  - Rollover equity for vets usually sits in the MSO or its parent. North Carolina's stalled SB 570 would have barred professionals from owning MSO shares for their own practices. — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/)
  - In NY-style regimes, "portions of the business can be owned by a non-licensed individual or entity, so long as the day to day clinical decisions are made by licensed veterinarians." — search summary of [RMF PC alert](https://rmfpc.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Veterinary-Law-Alert-Regulations-That-Govern-the-Sale-of-New-York-Veterinary-Practices.pdf)
- **Premises permits:** State schemes commonly require a licensed veterinarian named as the responsible on-site licensee, even where ownership is unrestricted. — [VeterinaryHires](https://www.veterinaryhires.com/career-guides/veterinary-practice-manager/practice-ownership/)

### Inferences
- **CT (her home state):** She could likely own the business through an LLC as long as a CT-licensed DVM is the responsible veterinarian and controls clinical decisions. This is inferred from the absence of a statutory ban in the sources reviewed. Pending private equity and corporate-practice bills make it a moving target. Counsel must confirm.
- **NY (e.g., Westchester):** Requires a vet-owned PC plus an MSO, often called the "friendly vet" model. The founder owns the MSO, which earns management fees and owns the real estate and equipment. This adds legal cost (likely tens of thousands of dollars in structuring; not sourced) and adds key-person risk: if the friendly DVM leaves, a stock-transfer or succession agreement is needed.
- **NJ and PA:** Assume the NY-style MSO is needed until counsel confirms otherwise.
- **DEA registration:** Under general federal law, each location dispensing controlled substances needs its own DEA registration held by a licensed practitioner, plus any state controlled-substance registration (CT DCP; NY BNE). Inferred from general knowledge; not sourced in this research.
- A lay owner cannot hold the DEA registration. That makes the DVM medical director indispensable.

### Gaps
- No authoritative 50-state table was found. The sources contradict each other on counts and state names.
- NJ statute and regulation text on ownership was not located.
- The current CT DPH premises-registration requirement and the final status of CT's 2025 proposals were not confirmed.
- Texas baseline ownership rule was not confirmed.
- MSO management fee levels (percent of revenue) were not found.
- AVMA policy on practice ownership was not fetched.

## 2. Startup capital: build-out, equipment, working capital, total, breakeven, SBA lending, urgent-care alternative

### Takeaway
No reliable published budget exists specifically for a 24/7 ER hospital. Most figures are general-practice or blog-template estimates.

Rough triangulation for a 4,000–8,000 sq ft leased ER hospital (an inference, not a sourced figure):
- Build-out, at about $225–350/sq ft for free-standing construction.
- Equipment in the hundreds of thousands of dollars.
- Working capital large enough to cover 12–24 months of overnight staffing losses.

That points to roughly $2–4M+ all-in. Urgent care (non-24/7) and franchise models run about $0.2–0.65M.

### Cited Findings

A sample 2,000 sq ft practice budgets about $75K for furniture and equipment and $30K for business systems. — [Bittsi startup cost calculator](https://bittsi.com/new-practice/startup-cost-calculator)
- **General-clinic template:** build-out $100K–$350K, diagnostic equipment $40K–$120K, working capital $50K–$150K. — [ZenBusiness](https://www.zenbusiness.com/start-a-business/animals/veterinary-clinic/)

- **Surgical/referral template:** $850K–$900K for surgical suite and build-out, and $250K for digital radiography. This is a single unverified template. — [businessplan-templates](https://businessplan-templates.com/blogs/start-business/veterinary-hospital)

## 3. Staffing for 24/7: DVMs, techs, recruiting, pay, bonuses, relief, equity

### Takeaway
ER veterinarians are scarce and expensive:
- Postings show $140K–$240K+ base, with Glassdoor's self-reported median total pay at about $290K.
- Sign-on bonuses for ER DVMs can reach $75K–$100K.
- Relief ER shifts run about $1,000–$1,800 each.

Staffing is the main operational risk.

### Cited Findings
- **Pay ranges from postings:** ZipRecruiter shows ER vet job pay of $176K–$205K and $176K–$234K. — [ZipRecruiter](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Jobs/Emergency-Veterinarian); [ZipRecruiter](https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Jobs/Emergency-And-Veterinarian-Salary)
- **Glassdoor:** self-reported median total pay for ER vets is about $290K/yr. The data may be about a year old. — [Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.com/Salaries/emergency-veterinarian-salary-SRCH_KO0,22.htm)
- **Salary.com:** national median of $115,145, which looks miscategorized; treat as unreliable. — [Salary.com](https://www.salary.com/research/salary/hiring/emergency-veterinarian-salary)
- **Sign-on bonuses:**
  - Example posting: Idaho Falls ER vet at $140K–$240K plus a $100K sign-on bonus. — search summary, [Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/q-emergency-veterinarian-jobs.html)
  - Oncology posting: up to $75K. — same search
  - Licensed vet tech (LVT) bonuses run up to about $3K. — [Cornell CVM jobs](https://cvmjobs.vet.cornell.edu/profile/orchard-park-veterinary-medical-center/2033225)
- **Relief pay:** ER shifts at $1,000–$1,800 per shift; overnight and holiday at $900–$1,500+; ER/metro hourly rates of $120–$170+. This is an anecdotal marketing blog. — [Serenity.vet](https://serenity.vet/blog/relief-veterinarian-salary-how-much-do-relief-vets-really-make/)
- **Staffing benchmarks:**
  - AVMA average practice: about 2.76 FTE DVMs, 5.74 techs and assistants, and 3.7 non-medical staff. — [AVMA](https://www.avma.org/news/benchmarking-data-plus-elevating-efficiency-equals-practice-productivity)
  - Optimal DVM-to-support ratio of about 1:4–1:5. — [bplan.ai](https://bplan.ai/blogs/review/veterinary-hospital-review)

### Inferences
- **DVM headcount for 24/7:** 24/7 single-doctor coverage is 168 hours a week. At about 36–40 clinical hours per FTE, that is roughly 4.5–5 FTE DVMs minimum. With double-coverage at peak (evenings and weekends), plus vacation and CE, realistic headcount is about 6–8 DVMs.
- **Tech headcount:** at 2–3 techs per DVM on shift, that implies roughly 12–20 tech FTEs plus CSRs.
- **DVM payroll:** at about $200–250K plus benefits, 6–8 DVMs is about $1.5–2.2M a year.
- **Recruiting incentives:** equity in the MSO, or a partnership in the PC, is the main way independents compete with Mars/VEG/NVA. VEG itself is known for a doctor-partnership model (not sourced here).
- **Urgent care is easier to staff:** non-24/7 hours (e.g., 12 noon–10pm, 7 days) reduce the need to about 3–4 DVMs and avoid overnight pay.

### Gaps
- No authoritative AVMA or recruiter survey on ER DVM comp or relief rates for 2025–26 was retrieved.
- No Northeast-specific figures were found.

## 5. Risks

### Takeaway
The biggest risks are:
- Staffing failure: being unable to fill overnight DVM shifts forces closures.
- Ramp-up losses under heavy fixed costs.
- Regulatory and structural risk in corporate-practice states.
- Competition from consolidators.
- Pricing backlash, which is common in ER vet care.

### Cited Findings
- Consolidation and private equity are drawing regulatory and antitrust scrutiny: FTC orders, NY A9042, Colorado SB 25-198, Texas SB 2130. — [Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/); [Texas SB 2130](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB02130I.htm); [Stateline](https://stateline.org/2024/03/29/vets-fret-as-private-equity-snaps-up-clinics-pet-care-companies/)
- MSO arrangements draw scrutiny when management directs clinicians or controls records. — [VeterinaryHires](https://www.veterinaryhires.com/career-guides/veterinary-practice-manager/practice-ownership/)
- Revenue per exam room has been declining in real terms since 2020. — [AVMA](https://www.avma.org/news/benchmarking-data-plus-elevating-efficiency-equals-practice-productivity)

### Inferences
- **Malpractice:** veterinary malpractice awards are generally limited because pets are treated as property, but board complaints and reputational harm are common in ER. Insurance is required: professional liability, general liability, and workers' comp (needle sticks and bites).
- **Controlled substances:** opioids and ketamine require DEA logs and diversion controls, and the DVM registrant carries the liability.
- **Competition:** a Mars, VEG or BluePearl ER opening nearby could quickly undercut an independent.
- **Pricing complaints:** ER invoices commonly run into the thousands, and online reviews drive volume.
- All of the above is general industry knowledge, not sourced here.

### Gaps
- No sourced data on ER vet malpractice claim frequency or insurance premiums.
- No sourced data on closure rates of independent ERs.

## 6. Synergies with mobile grooming and lower-capex adjacencies

### Takeaway
A mobile vet or wellness van is a far lower-capex adjacency than an ER:
- About $85–250K for the vehicle, plus $50–100K of equipment.
- One estimate puts all-in startup at $300–750K.
- Revenue of about $150–600K a year for solo-to-small-group operations.

It fits her existing van, routing and customer infrastructure. It still needs a licensed DVM, and in NY/NJ a vet-owned entity.

### Cited Findings
- **Mobile vet startup costs:** custom van $85K–$250K, equipment $50K–$100K, inventory $10K–$20K. Another guide gives $300K–$750K total. — [ZenBusiness mobile vet](https://www.zenbusiness.com/start-a-business/animals/mobile-vet/); [TRUiC](https://truicbusinessideas.com/mobile-vet-business); [Craftsmen Industries](https://www.craftsmenind.com/blog/veterinary-mobile-clinics-getting-started)
- **Mobile vet operating costs:** fuel $500–$1,000 a month, insurance $5K–$10K a year, maintenance $2K–$5K a year. — [ZenBusiness](https://www.zenbusiness.com/start-a-business/animals/mobile-vet/)
- **Mobile vet revenue:**
  - About $150K–$600K a year. A hypothetical example: 5 visits a day at $250 = $312K a year. — [TRUiC](https://truicbusinessideas.com/mobile-vet-business); [Beancount](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/08/29/mobile-veterinary-practice-bookkeeping-per-visit-profit-guide)
  - Mobile visits run $100–$300+, versus $50–$150 at clinics. — [Digitail](https://digitail.com/blog/the-pros-and-cons-of-being-a-mobile-vet/); [Provet](https://www.provet.com/blog/how-to-start-a-mobile-veterinary-practice-tips-for-success)
- **Mobile vet profit and breakeven:** example net profit of $61K–$239K a year, and breakeven in 6–18 months (a guide estimate). Drive time limits capacity. — [TRUiC](https://truicbusinessideas.com/mobile-vet-business); [Provet](https://www.provet.com/blog/how-to-start-a-mobile-veterinary-practice-tips-for-success)
- **Mobile vs. clinic:** mobile avoids rent and most facility costs; one comparison puts a clinic at about $1M. — [Digitail](https://digitail.com/blog/the-pros-and-cons-of-being-a-mobile-vet/)

### Inferences
- **Groomer synergies:**
  - Her grooming client list is a ready marketing base for a vet service.
  - Groomers often spot skin, ear and lump issues and can refer clients on.
  - Shared booking (MoeGo), routing, fleet maintenance and van-upfit vendor relationships.
  - Bundled "groom + vaccine" days.
- **Lower-capex sequence:**
  1. Partner with a mobile or relief DVM for vaccine/wellness clinics at grooming stops or in community pop-ups. Very low capex.
  2. Telehealth triage partnership. Note that the vet-client-patient relationship (VCPR) rules limit tele-prescribing and vary by state.
  3. One mobile wellness van with a DVM partner (~$150–350K).
  4. Daytime/evening urgent care (~$0.3–1M).
  5. Full 24/7 ER only after building DVM relationships and data on volumes.
- **Legal caution:** in NY/NJ, even a mobile vet or vaccine clinic must be clinically run by a vet-owned entity. The grooming company could provide vans, booking and marketing under an MSO-style services agreement at fair-market-value fees. That needs counsel.
