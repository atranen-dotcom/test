# VEG built the pet ER. Don't clone it yet.

Short answer, Alicia: Veterinary Emergency Group (now branded "VEG ER for Pets") is a real success story. It grew from one White Plains ER hospital in 2014 to **"over 130" walk-in, 24/7 emergency hospitals in 36 states, DC and Ontario by October 2026**, backed by roughly **$150M of disclosed venture equity** from Sequoia Heritage, D1, Fidelity and Durable. Even so, starting a VEG-style 24/7 ER yourself, even in an "underserved" area, is not a good idea for you right now. A single hospital plausibly needs **$2.5–4.5M all-in** (an estimate; nobody publishes a reliable figure), takes 18–36 months to break even (also an estimate), and depends on hiring six to eight emergency veterinarians who can each command roughly **$150K–$325K base pay** in a famously short-staffed specialty. In New York, and probably New Jersey, a non-vet can't legally own the practice at all without a vet-owned entity plus a management company. The underserved areas are underserved largely *because* nobody can staff them overnight, and that problem doesn't care how good your marketing is. The version that fits you is smaller and cheaper. Partner with a licensed DVM through a management-services (MSO) structure to add vaccine and wellness services to your grooming routes, maybe graduating to a mobile vet van ($150–350K). Then decide on daytime urgent care only once you've hit real milestones. Meanwhile, the cleanest way to own a piece of the VEG thesis is as the public-markets investor you already are. That means watching for an eventual VEG IPO, which is plausible but completely unconfirmed, and in the meantime looking at listed pet-health names. A quick note on sourcing: facts below carry citations. Anything labeled *estimate* or *inference* is my arithmetic or judgment, not a reported number.

## One ER vet and one finance guy took a White Plains clinic to 130+ hospitals

VEG was founded in **2014 by Dr. David Bessler, VMD**, who had been an emergency vet since 2003. He bought a single ER hospital in White Plains, NY ([Retail Insider](https://retail-insider.com/retail-insider/2026/02/veg-er-for-pets-opens-etobicoke-emergency-hospital-as-part-of-north-american-expansion/)). His co-founder and president, **David Glattstein**, is an Emory and Wharton MBA with a private-equity background who drove the fundraising and roll-out ([Emory Business](https://www.emorybusiness.com/2023/08/09/meet-david-glattstein-changing-the-game-for-veterinary-emergency-care/)). Keep that pairing in mind, because it matters later: a clinician who controls the medicine, plus a capital allocator who builds the machine. Bessler is still CEO as of 2026, and headquarters remain in White Plains ([BBB](https://www.bbb.org/us/ny/white-plains/profile/emergency-vet/veterinary-emergency-group-0121-87139337)). The leadership bench has filled out. VEG named the industry's first Chief Veterinary Nursing Officer in 2021 ([dvm360](https://www.dvm360.com/view/veterinary-emergency-group-appoints-chief-veterinary-nursing-officer)) and a first-of-its-kind Chief Quality Officer, Dr. Meredith Daly, in January 2025 ([dvm360](https://www.dvm360.com/view/paws-and-profits-new-leadership)). It also lost operating talent. Kristen Lombardi, who helped grow VEG "from one clinic with 13 employees to 100 clinics and 5,500 team members," left in March 2025 to become COO of Small Door ([Patch](https://patch.com/new-york/new-york-city/small-door-veterinary-appoints-first-ever-chief-operating-officer)).

The model is deliberately narrow and a bit radical. VEG does **emergency and urgent care only, 24/7/365, walk-in, no appointments, no general practice and no specialty referral line**. Its signature is the **open-concept hospital**: exam tables and doctors' desks sit in the middle, kennels line the perimeter, and owners can stay with their pet through treatment, *including surgery and overnight stays*. Callers reach a licensed vet on the phone, and triage starts on arrival before any forms ([Pembroke Pines FL News](https://www.pembrokepinesflnews.com/news/local/article316548391.html); [Today's Veterinary Business](https://todaysveterinarybusiness.com/pets-open-concept-veterinary-hospitals/)). Services include surgery, endoscopy, ultrasound, X-ray, transfusions and hospitalization, for everything from dogs to turtles but not venomous species or primates ([Retail Insider](https://retail-insider.com/retail-insider/2026/02/veg-er-for-pets-opens-etobicoke-emergency-hospital-as-part-of-north-american-expansion/)). The real estate formula is just as repeatable: roughly **5,000 sq ft in retail strips and power centers**. The first Canadian site sits beside a Best Buy and a Home Depot. My inference is that the free vet phone line doubles as triage and customer acquisition. I found no evidence of a paid telehealth product or app.

The expansion has been almost entirely **de novo** (built from scratch, not acquired). No acquisitions turned up beyond the original 2014 purchase.

The gap between ambition and pace hints at what limits VEG too: staffing, not demand. ## $150M raised and a claimed $500M in revenue, but the unit economics stay private

The verified funding history is short. **Sequoia Heritage** invested in VEG's first two rounds starting in 2017. In **September 2021, D1 Capital Partners and Fidelity co-led a $100M third round**, with Durable Capital Partners participating, which brought total equity to about **$150M** ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20210922005550/en/)). The money was earmarked, notably, for people rather than buildings: recruitment, vet school-debt repayment, credential support, nursing salaries and well-being. PitchBook lists total funding of $111M (which conflicts with the company's figure) and shows the latest deal type as **debt** ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/company/252217-72)). A blog claims a $500M round in 2024 at a $2.5B+ valuation, but it names no source and nothing corroborates it, so ignore it. *Inference:* growing from about 50 to 130+ sites without a new announced equity round implies funding from operating cash flow plus undisclosed debt. That would mean the hospitals throw off real cash. It's also why the 2021 crossover-fund investors (D1, Fidelity, Durable are the classic pre-IPO crowd) make an eventual IPO or sponsor sale plausible. **No IPO filing or sale has been reported.**

On operations, VEG said in 2023 that revenue **"exceeds $500 million"** at 53 hospitals, with 3,500+ employees and 1M+ pets helped ([Emory Business](https://www.emorybusiness.com/2023/08/09/meet-david-glattstein-changing-the-game-for-veterinary-emergency-care/)). Put on your investor hat here. That's a company-supplied number in an alumni magazine. It implies about **$9–10M per hospital** (*estimate*), which is very high for a 5,000 sq ft box, so it's probably a run rate or gross charges. Staffing works out to roughly **55–65 people per hospital** (*estimate*). Extrapolating to 130 sites gives a 2026 revenue range of very roughly $650M–$1.3B. That is pure arithmetic, not a reported figure. Per-site EBITDA, average ticket and payback period are not public anywhere.

Reputation splits along a predictable line. Customers rate VEG highly by its own account: **Google 4.7 average and NPS 91** (company-reported, 2023) ([Emory Business](https://www.emorybusiness.com/2023/08/09/meet-david-glattstein-changing-the-game-for-veterinary-emergency-care/)). Only "three or four people out of 2,000" staff reportedly disliked the open floor plan ([Today's Veterinary Business](https://todaysveterinarybusiness.com/pets-open-concept-veterinary-hospitals/)). The criticism centers on money. VEG requires upfront payment. Bessler refused to install Trupanion's insurer direct-pay software, warning that insurance would "ruin the veterinary industry," and when asked about clients who can't pay upfront he said, "I don't have a solution for that" ([The Canine Review](https://thecaninereview.com/2023/01/30/veterinary-emergency-group-ceo-attacks-pet-insurers-offers-no-solution-for-pet-parents-who-cant-pay-vet-bills-up-front/)). There are a handful of lawsuits, all unproven allegations: a vet tech's 2025 retaliation suit in Washington ([Law.com](https://www.law.com/radar/card/wa-snohomishcounty-651282-rickstrew-v-veterinary-emergency-group)) and a Tampa-area owner alleging payment was demanded before treating a pregnant dog whose puppies died ([Tampa Free Press](https://www.tampafp.com/valuable-puppies-allegedly-die-in-the-hands-of-veterinarian/)). On the softer side, VEG has started a 2026 Maddie's Fund pilot (listed at $750K) to expand access to surgical care ([Maddie's Fund](https://www.maddiesfund.org/veg.htm)). That could be an early step toward lower-cost care options, though that reading is my speculation.

## A $42B industry growing on price while running short on overnight staff

The backdrop is a large, mature market. Americans spent about **$158B on pets in 2025**, and **vet care was about $41B, up only 3%** ([GlobalPETS/APPA](https://globalpetindustry.com/news/158b-us-pet-industry-maintains-steady-growth-through-2025/)). Here's the uncomfortable part: that growth is price, not volume. **Vet visits fell 2.3% in 2024**, the average gap between visits stretched from about 73 days to more than 112, pet-services prices are **up 42% since 2019**, and **52% of owners have skipped or declined care because of cost** ([Octus](https://octus.com/resources/articles/private-credit-exposure-to-veterinary-rollups-shows-growing-dispersion-vsos-under-increasing-pressure/)). Visit volumes have fallen four years running since 2022 ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-states/industry/emergency-veterinary-services-in-the-us/4025/)). Pet insurance is growing fast but covers only **4.27% of US pets** ([dvm360/NAPHIA](https://www.dvm360.com/view/us-pet-insurance-enrollment-increased-9-in-2025-but-more-than-95-of-cats-and-dogs-remain-uninsured)), so nearly every ER bill comes out of the owner's own pocket. *Inference:* emergencies can't be postponed the way a wellness visit can, which makes ER the most resilient corner of the industry. But that cost sensitivity caps how high invoices can climb. (Nobody has a reliable dollar size for the ER segment; IBISWorld's two figures, $2.3B and $49.5B, are roughly 20x apart.)

Emergency and specialty care is already heavily corporate: **about 75% of specialty and emergency clinics are corporate-owned**, versus 25–50% of general practices ([Octus](https://octus.com/resources/articles/private-credit-exposure-to-veterinary-rollups-shows-growing-dispersion-vsos-under-increasing-pressure/)). The big players are Mars, which owns **BluePearl (110+ hospitals in 30 states)** plus VCA and Banfield ([Mars](https://www.mars.com/news-and-stories/press-releases-statements/bluepearl-announces-new-hospital-monroeville)); **Ethos** (145 specialty hospitals at its 2023 spin-out from NVA) ([Ethos](https://www.ethosvet.com/news-article/new-hospital-network-known-as-ethos-veterinary-health/)); and Thrive, PetVet, MedVet and others. BluePearl has even opened a stand-alone urgent-care hospital ([BluePearl](https://bluepearlvet.com/press-releases-announcements/bluepearl-opens-first-ever-stand-alone-urgent-care-hospital/)), so the giants are moving down-market into the space a newcomer might target.

The cautionary tales are useful. The heavily leveraged private-equity roll-ups are struggling. **Thrive Pet Healthcare** needed a 2025 distressed exchange that extended **$1.7B+ of debt** after citing labor costs, staffing shortages and falling volumes ([Thrive](https://www.thrivepetcare.com/thrive-guide/thrive-pet-healthcare-secures-new-financing); [Octus](https://octus.com/?p=45673)). Lenders marked down loans to **PetVet (88% of par)** and **AmeriVet (83%)** ([Octus](https://octus.com/resources/articles/private-credit-exposure-to-veterinary-rollups-shows-growing-dispersion-vsos-under-increasing-pressure/)). Venture-backed urgent-care and "modern GP" chains are consolidating: **Bond Vet and Small Door merged in July 2026** into a 55-clinic network ([dvm360](https://www.dvm360.com/view/bond-vet-and-small-door-veterinary-merge-to-form-55-clinic-network)), and Bond has closed its Rye Brook site near you ([Private Equity Vet](https://privateequityvet.org/changelog/)). The bright spot: loans to specialty and ER businesses were marked at or above par. *Inference:* ER economics are holding up better than general practice. That's good for VEG and BluePearl, and it's exactly why they'll keep moving into the best markets.

Valuations explain why investors keep piling in. Private ER clinics trade around **10.4x EBITDA** in the $5–10M EBITDA band and specialists around 13.2x ([First Page Sage](https://firstpagesage.com/business/veterinary-practice-ebitda-valuation-multiples/)). Scaled platforms are richer still: **Mission Pet Health at roughly 14.8x** ($8.6B on about $580M EBITDA) and Western Veterinary Partners at a "high-teens" multiple ([Octus](https://octus.com/resources/articles/private-credit-exposure-to-veterinary-rollups-shows-growing-dispersion-vsos-under-increasing-pressure/)). Regulators are paying attention. The FTC made Mars sell 12 specialty and ER clinics to approve its VCA purchase in 2017 ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2017/12/ftc-alters-final-consent-order-response-public-comments-preserving-competition-specialty-emergency)). The UK's competition authority finalized mandatory online price lists and ownership disclosure for vets in March 2026 ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/what-veterinary-businesses-and-vets-need-to-do-following-the-cmas-final-vets-report)). Colorado now requires 60 days' notice before veterinary deals close, and New York has a similar bill pending ([Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/who-can-legally-own-a-veterinary-practice/); [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2026-01-14-no-paws-oversight-will-new-yorks-proposed-veterinary)). *Inference:* ER pricing, which produces the biggest out-of-pocket bills, is the most exposed if US price-transparency rules arrive.

The real choke point is people. Whether there's an overall vet shortage is genuinely disputed. An AVMA-commissioned forecast says supply is adequate through 2035 ([AVMA](https://www.avma.org/news/no-dire-shortage-veterinarians-anticipated-coming-years)), while the vet schools' association projects only **about 76% of demand met by 2032** ([AAVMC](https://www.aavmc.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Demand-for-and-Supply-of-Veterinarians-in-the-U.S.-to-2032-New.pdf)). The *overnight* ER shortage, though, shows up everywhere. The University of Minnesota's animal hospital now closes midnight to 8am four nights a week for lack of overnight techs ([Yahoo](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/u-m-vet-clinic-reduces-204552817.html)). Monroe County, NY's only 24/7 ER routinely turns pets away ([Rochester First](https://www.rochesterfirst.com/news/no-quick-fixes-monroe-countys-only-24-7-emergency-vet-short-staffed/)). In Door County, WI, the nearest ER is **1–3 hours away** ([Wisconsin Watch](https://wisconsinwatch.org/2026/08/wisconsin-door-county-veterinarian-shortage-clinics-dog-cat-pet-owners-emergency-care/)). 2026 job postings offer ER vets **$150K–$325K base for about three 12-hour shifts a week** ([iHire](https://ihireveterinary.com/jobs/view/466087575)), with sign-on bonuses up to $100K ([Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/q-emergency-veterinarian-jobs.html)). So yes, the "ER deserts" you're picturing are real. They exist mostly because nobody can staff an overnight hospital there, not because nobody has thought to open one.

## A VEG-style ER costs $2.5–4.5M and lives or dies on six to eight ER vets

Here's what it would take for you, specifically, to do this. First, the legal question. Ownership rules differ by state and sources disagree on the count; roughly 9 to 18 states restrict non-vet ownership ([VeterinaryHires](https://www.veterinaryhires.com/career-guides/veterinary-practice-manager/practice-ownership/); [Economic Liberties](https://www.economicliberties.us/our-work/the-save-our-pets-act-stopping-the-corporate-takeover-of-veterinary-practices/)). **New York is unambiguous**: only a vet-owned professional service corporation may practice veterinary medicine (Educ. Law § 6706) ([NY Senate](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/EDN/6706)). New Jersey and Pennsylvania are usually listed as restrictive, but I couldn't confirm either from the statute text. For **Connecticut I found no statutory ban** on lay ownership, but the state's facility regulations are written around vet-operated hospitals ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/connecticut/Regs-Conn-State-Agencies-SS-20-196-1)), and private-equity restrictions keep being proposed. The standard workaround is the **MSO model**. A vet-owned professional corporation holds the medicine: clinical decisions, records and the vets' employment. Your company owns the real estate, equipment, staffing administration, billing and marketing, and earns a management fee. That structure draws scrutiny when the management side steers clinical care ([VeterinaryHires](https://www.veterinaryhires.com/career-guides/veterinary-practice-manager/practice-ownership/)). *Inference:* the controlled-substance (DEA) registration would also have to sit with a licensed vet, so your medical director isn't a nice-to-have. They're the license that keeps the doors open. All of this needs confirmation from veterinary-transaction counsel in each state.

Second, the capital. There's no credible published budget for a 24/7 ER, so this is triangulated from contractor and template sources, all of which are *estimates*. Free-standing vet construction runs about **$225–350/sq ft** ([Bittsi](https://bittsi.com/new-practice/startup-cost-calculator)), and full ER/specialty hospitals reach **$400–700/sq ft** ([Terrapin CG](https://terrapincg.com/news/average-cost-to-build-veterinary-clinic-usa)). Equipment adds **$300K–$1M** ([Nidana](https://nidana.io/blog/cost-to-open-veterinary-clinic)).

| Item (6,000 sq ft leased, *estimate*) | Range |
|---|---|
| Build-out at $225–350/sq ft | ~$1.35–2.1M |
| ER equipment (digital X-ray, ultrasound, lab, anesthesia, oxygen/ICU, surgical) | ~$0.4–0.8M |
| Working capital to fund 12–24 months of overnight losses | ~$0.5–1.5M |
| **All-in** | **~$2.5–4.5M** |

Greenwich-area construction would land at the top of that range. SBA 7(a) loans go up to $5M, and Live Oak Bank started out as a veterinary lender ([Live Oak](https://www.liveoak.bank/business-loans/sba-loans/?RefId=blog)). *Inference:* a lender would look hard at a non-vet founder with no veterinary operating history, so expect to need a DVM partner, substantial equity and a personal guarantee.

Third, the people, which is where it really gets hard. 24/7 coverage is 168 hours a week. With one doctor always on, peak-hour double coverage, vacations and continuing education, that means about **6–8 ER vets** plus **12–20 technicians** (*estimate*). At $200–250K per vet, vet payroll alone runs about **$1.5–2.2M a year** before a single tech or receptionist (*estimate*). Glassdoor puts median self-reported total pay for ER vets near **$290K** ([Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.com/Salaries/emergency-veterinarian-salary-SRCH_KO0,22.htm)), and relief shifts cost about **$1,000–1,800** each ([Serenity.vet](https://serenity.vet/blog/relief-veterinarian-salary-how-much-do-relief-vets-really-make/)). Revenue can be big: the median ER invoice is about **$808** ([VetReceipt](https://vetreceipt.com/vet-care-cost)), so 25–40 cases a day works out to roughly **$7–12M a year** (*estimate*). Reported ER margins run 15–25% ([Transitions Elite](https://transitionselite.com/vet-practice-owner-salary/)). The catch is that the overnight cost base is fixed from day one while volume ramps slowly, and in the rural "desert" markets you'd be recruiting vets to exactly the kind of small town that, per AVMA, couldn't compete on pay even back in 2019 ([AVMA](https://www.avma.org/News/JAVMANews/Pages/190301a.aspx)).

Put it together and here's the candid verdict. VEG's founding team was an ER vet plus a PE-trained capital raiser. You're one half of that pair, and unfortunately it's not the half that can hold a DEA registration. The playbook only works if the clinical half is a deeply committed partner, not an employee you're hoping stays.

## The better play: add vet services to your grooming routes, partner with a DVM through an MSO, and invest

Your real advantages are a fleet, routing, a booking system and a loyal, affluent client base that already lets you handle their dogs. Those fit the *low-capex, daytime* end of veterinary care. A mobile vet van costs about **$85–250K** for the vehicle plus **$50–100K** of equipment ([ZenBusiness](https://www.zenbusiness.com/start-a-business/animals/mobile-vet/)). Mobile visits bill **$100–300+**, versus $50–150 in a clinic ([Digitail](https://digitail.com/blog/the-pros-and-cons-of-being-a-mobile-vet/)). Guide estimates put revenue at $150–600K a year and breakeven at **6–18 months** ([TRUiC](https://truicbusinessideas.com/mobile-vet-business); [Provet](https://www.provet.com/blog/how-to-start-a-mobile-veterinary-practice-tips-for-success)). Treat those as optimistic. Even so, the downside of a mobile van is a used van, while the downside of a failed ER is a $3M hole and a lease you can't get out of. In ER terms, one hospital's budget buys somewhere between **10 and 50 mobile vet vans** (*estimate*).

| Option | Capital (*estimates* unless cited) | Fit with your business | Main risk | My take |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| VEG-style 24/7 ER | ~$2.5–4.5M | Low: new customers, new skills | Overnight staffing, ramp losses, VEG/BluePearl competition | **No, not now** |
| Daytime/evening urgent care (e.g., noon–10pm) with DVM partner | ~$0.3–1M; franchises $190K–$655K ([FDD Exchange](https://fddexchange.com/fdd/petsmart-veterinary-services-2024/); [Franchise Payback](https://www.franchisepayback.com/franchise/petwellclinic)) | Medium | Still needs 3–4 DVMs, local competition | **Maybe, later, after milestones** |
| Mobile vet/wellness van via MSO with a DVM partner | ~$150–350K | High: same vans, routes, clients, booking | Finding the right DVM, state VCPR and ownership rules | **Best "operator" option** |
| Vaccine/wellness days at grooming stops with a relief DVM | Very low | Very high | Legal structure; small revenue | **Start here as a cheap test** |
| Invest (pet-health equities; a VEG IPO if one happens) | Your choice | Uses your investing skills | Valuation, liquidity | **Yes, alongside** |

The sequence I'd suggest starts with your core business. Finish the sixth van and settle the salon decision first, because a vet venture shouldn't starve a business that's already working. Next, hire veterinary-transaction counsel for a one-time CT/NY review of what your grooming company can legally do as a service provider to a vet-owned practice. Then run a **six-month pilot**. Partner with a relief or mobile DVM who owns the clinical entity. Your company supplies booking, routing, a van and marketing at fair-market fees, and offers "groom + vaccine" days and wellness checks to your existing clients. *Inference:* your groomers already spot ears, skin issues and lumps every day, and that's a natural referral funnel no ER chain has. Set clear kill-or-scale criteria before you start, for example a target share of clients booking a vet service, gross margin per vet day, and whether your DVM partner wants equity and a long-term role. If the pilot works, add a dedicated mobile vet van. Only after you've built real DVM relationships and your own demand data should daytime urgent care in an exurban market (where existing ERs are overloaded) even come up. A 24/7 ER stays off the table unless an emergency-trained vet shows up wanting to be your Bessler, and even then it would be their hospital with your management company behind it.

On the investing side, the private operators (Mars, VEG, NVA/Ethos) aren't directly accessible. Listed exposure includes IDEXX (diagnostics), Zoetis (animal health), Chewy (which runs Chewy Vet Care clinics), Petco (Vetco), Trupanion (insurance) and Elanco. I pulled no current valuation data, so treat this as a list to research, not a recommendation. VEG's pre-IPO investor roster makes it worth a watchlist entry, but no listing has been reported.

## Conclusion

The most useful lesson from VEG is about the shape of a winning vet business, not about emergency medicine itself. VEG's moat isn't its open floor plan or its strip-mall leases, which anyone can copy. It's the ability to recruit and keep emergency clinicians at scale, which is why its 2021 money went to school-debt repayment and nurse pay rather than real estate, and why even VEG is opening 25–30 hospitals a year instead of the 100+ a year its 500-site goal implied. Underserved areas look like gaps in the market, but mostly they're gaps in staffing, and an outsider with less capital and no clinical network starts out behind on exactly the thing that matters most.

For you, the asymmetric bet points the other way. Bring a licensed vet *into* the routes, clients and logistics you already have, keep capital and lease risk small, and let real data (not industry averages) tell you whether to scale. If it works, you'll have built the clinician relationships and local demand data that a bigger move would need. If it doesn't, you've lost a few months and a modest check, and your dogs still leave looking fabulous.
